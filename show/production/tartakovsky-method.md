# The Tartakovsky method — wordless storytelling, distilled from his own interviews

All quotes Genndy Tartakovsky unless noted. Sources (fetched
2026-10-27): Cartoon Brew interview (cartoonbrew.com); The Dot and
Line interview (dotandline.net); Toonado interview summary
(toonado.com); Collider interview (collider.com); Television Academy
feature (televisionacademy.com); Animation Obsessive, "The Visual
World of Samurai Jack" + "How They Painted the Samurai Jack
Backgrounds" (animationobsessive.substack.com).

## 1. Slowness is the technique
- "Animation is too fast. I wanted to slow things down." (Cartoon
  Brew headline, his thesis.)
- On the Jack → Primal progression: "As we progressed into Jack, we
  were like, 'I'm going to slow things down a little bit.' I really
  wanted to get the audience to *feel*. Instead of just a pan of a
  background, I wanted you to get sucked in and really look at the
  environment… With Primal, I'm like, 'I'm going to slow it down even
  more.' Because I always feel like I'm not giving enough room for the
  music or the sound effects."
- "In animation, it's all about cut, cut, cut — you have to keep
  things moving. I wanted to go against that grain, and trust the
  music, drawings, and story to let people in."
- The Jack pilot screening story (Animation Obsessive): two minutes of
  wordless action and atmosphere; the first episode "was so silent…
  complete silence" in the exec screening — the room's word was
  "speechless," and the experiment was greenlit on that silence.

## 2. Action is composed like music
- "With an action sequence, I just think about music and rhythm. With
  any good piece of music, there's highs and lows, fast and slow:
  'fight, fight, fight… slow… fight, fight, fight.'"
- To CBR (via Looper): action choreographed "rhythmically… fast-fast-
  fast-slow or slow-slow-slow-fast — any kind of rhythm you can
  establish." Failure mode: "when you're doing things haphazardly,
  and there's not a plan."

## 3. The environment is a character, given real minutes
- On his film influences (Lawrence of Arabia, Kurosawa, Dr. Zhivago,
  Miyazaki): they "really made the background a character… I wanted
  to do, maybe, a minute or two sequence of just establishing the
  environment. You really get to feel where Jack is… like watching
  Lawrence of Arabia, you really *feel* that desert."
- His observation about audiences: "the things people react to the
  most are the really visceral sequences — the ones with just sound
  effects or music." Primal began as the question: "Could we make a
  whole show or movie with just those sequences put together?"
- No-dialogue dividend (Television Academy): "People started to read
  their own feelings into it without being told how to feel… The
  subtlety of everything became heightened."

## 4. Sound is half the picture — and it is designed per-creature
- Sound designer Joel Valentine has worked with Tartakovsky since
  before Dexter's Lab. On Fang: "I didn't want just the normal roars.
  She really developed her own sound, with the little clicks and
  moans, which brought her to life."
- Music is written AFTER picture, cut to length, scored sequence by
  sequence against pacing and mood (Tyler Bates + Joanne Higginbottom
  on Primal; Bates also did Jack S5).
- His early doubt and its resolution (Collider): watching silent cuts
  he worried "Did I make a huge mistake?" — "once we started to fill
  it in with sound effects and music… him just screaming and
  breathing… the show became emotional."

## 5. Creature acting = pose libraries and eyes, not gesture
- Spear: "Because there's no dialogue, there's no gesturing. It was
  really more about, 'How do I pose him?' The eyes, all his
  expressions, were very important."
- Fang: a deliberate anti-cartoon rule — build "the whole library of
  poses and angles on her face," keep her "more of a beast."
- Episode generation: start from "a tonal idea, or a mood… what I
  wanted to feel here," then the relationship beat; designs follow.

## 6. The background pipeline (Samurai Jack) — Scott Wills' color keys
From Animation Obsessive's Wills interviews:
- Art director Scott Wills "touched every Samurai Jack background to
  some degree" and set the painting style; Dan Krall did black-and-
  white background layouts ("flat, sort of a surreal look," perspective
  ignored then restored for specific shots).
- Method: Wills + Tartakovsky walked each episode's storyboard and
  picked which shots needed a **color key** — a small, high-detail
  painting that the overseas painters (Rough Draft Korea) had to match
  exactly: "I want the finished paintings to look exactly like the
  keys." Average episode: ~30 keys, sometimes 50+. Wills personally
  scanned and color-corrected every key (the crew called him "The
  Wizard," working inside a black curtain against light pollution).
- Style sources: Hanna-Barbera grammar blended with "Japanese prints
  and posters," Charley Harper as the supreme influence for Wills;
  1950s Disney, UPA, Miyazaki on Tartakovsky's side.
- Wills' own technique tutorials (cel-vinyl, airbrush, paper-towel
  texture) are on YouTube — four short videos, uploaded from 2010.

## 7. What does NOT exist publicly (honest scarcity)
- No published Samurai Jack series bible. No comprehensive official
  art-of book for Jack or Primal surfaced in this search. Storyboards
  exist as individual artists' portfolio pieces and occasional auction
  lots, not as a public archive. The public record of the *method* is
  interviews (above) + fan-wiki transcripts/quote records + the Wills
  background coverage. Anyone selling a "complete Jack production
  archive" is selling something that does not exist as a public item.
